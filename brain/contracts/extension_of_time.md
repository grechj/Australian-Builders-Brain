An extension of time (EOT) moves the contract completion date for a delay
the builder isn't responsible for. Without one, the builder risks owing the
client liquidated damages for finishing late.

# Common qualifying delays

Check the contract's actual list; these are typical.

- Inclement weather and its after-effects (wet ground, drying time)
- Variations
- Client delays: late selections, late payments, access
- Authority delays: approvals, inspections, service connections
- Latent conditions
- Industrial action, or a supplier shortage outside the builder's control

# What makes a claim stick

1. **Notify on time.** Contracts set a notice period counted from when the builder became aware of the delay. Missing it can lose the claim. Find the period in the contract ([clause map](/contracts/hia_mba_clause_map.md)) and put it in the project file.
2. **Put it in writing** with the [Delay Notification Letter](/documents/delay_notification_letter.md): cause, dates, days claimed, revised completion date.
3. **Keep evidence.** [Site diary](/documents/site_diary.md) entries for every lost day, weather records, emails, and authority correspondence.
4. **Show the critical path.** Explain why the delay pushes out completion, not just one activity.

# Delay costs

Some contracts let the builder claim costs (site overheads, hire) for
delays caused by the client or authorities. It is a separate entitlement
from the time itself. Check the contract.
